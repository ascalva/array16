Verdict. A 3-D layout of the whips buys one thing a horizontal plate cannot: elevation for sources within about 15-20° of the horizon, and with it the ability to separate or null two sources at the same azimuth but different elevations. It does not improve azimuth, it is not the cure for today's elevated-source failures, and outdoors ground reflection erodes its elevation advantage. For the panel, 3-D means more faces: roughly one tile per 90-120° of azimuth.

Why the plate is weak near the horizon. A horizontal array measures only the direction cosines (u, v) = cos(el)(cos az, sin az); d(rho)/d(el) = -sin(el) vanishes at the horizon, so the elevation CRB of any planar array diverges in the array plane while a 3-D array's stays finite (Vu et al.; Gazzah and Marcos; Baysal and Moses; Nielsen). For the two-ring plate at 1 GHz (20 dB per whip, 64 samples) the elevation CRB is 1.4/0.55/0.28/0.15° at 2/5/10/20° up, six times that with the 3° calibration residual, and 2-9° at the horizon (square-root law). Raising the inner ring 300 mm, or stacking rings, flattens it to 0.04° (noise) / 0.24° (calibration). The sibling simulation agrees: elevation p95 for sources 0-10° up falls from 7.35/4.39° to 0.80/0.31° (0.4-1/1-2.3 GHz).

Azimuth does not change, because it depends only on the horizontal spread: raised-ring and planar CRBs are identical (0.048° noise, 0.29° calibration at 1 GHz), and the project's GA found 3-D and planar layouts with the same azimuth p95 (about 0.52°). With a fixed element count, height takes aperture away (Vu et al.).

Today's failure is an estimator mismatch. Assuming el = 0 leaves a ring of radius r with a phase error k r (1 - cos el): 72° for the 225 mm ring at 2 GHz and 30° up, a main-lobe loss of 2.4/3.9/5.4 dB at 1.6/2/2.3 GHz, while the single 8-ring's competing peaks are only 0.7-1.7 dB down. A joint azimuth-elevation search on the existing plate removes the mismatch (0 % gross errors up to 60° in the sibling run) without adding ambiguity (competing peaks -5.4/-3.6/-2.2 dB at 1/2.3/6 GHz either way). Commercial correlative interferometers work this way.

Costs of 3-D. (1) Ground reflection: a horizontal plate is first-order immune (the image has the same u, v); vertical aperture meets Barton's low-angle problem, an estimated 2-9° elevation bias at 0.4-1 GHz for sources 3-20° up over average ground. (2) Two levels 300 mm apart alias in elevation above 0.5-1 GHz; competing peaks rise from -3.6 to -1.5 dB at 2.3 GHz. (3) Raised whips lose the plate as ground: sleeve dipoles with chokes, cables through the array, elevation-dependent patterns, a manifold measured over azimuth and elevation. Random-array sidelobes stay near 1/N in any dimension (Lo; Steinberg).

Beamforming. Nulling an interferer 20° above a horizon source at the same azimuth costs the plate 24/16/9 dB of the wanted signal at 0.4/1/2.3 GHz, against 7.5/1.1/3.4 dB with the inner ring raised 300 mm.

Commercial practice. Narda's ADFA 2 stacks two 9-element rings for two bands and reports azimuth and elevation; R&S stacks per-band rings on one mast and computes elevation in its correlative interferometer; KrakenSDR gives bearings only. Stacked rings serve bands, not vertical aperture.

Panel. Its boresight is horizontal, so it already measures elevation near the horizon. Full 360° needs 3 tiles (±60°, 3.9 dB scan loss at the edges) or 4 (±45°, 2.0 dB): about $1,050-1,570 more, still one 16-element tile per direction, channels switched between faces (AN/SPY-1 uses four faces, PAVE PAWS two of ±60°). A turntable covers 360° in sequence.

Polarisation is a separate axis: everything here is V-pol. Dual-pol elements or vector sensors (Nehorai and Paldi) address it; 3-D placement does not.

The VHF/UHF DF system stacks ADD153 (20-1300 MHz, 9 elements), ADD050 (20-200 MHz, 9 elements on a 3 m circle) and ADD070 (1.3-3 GHz, 8-element circle, 340 mm) on one mast; ADD195 over ADD071 is another stack. The ADD253 has 9 VHF/UHF and 8 UHF elements. The HF 9-element circular arrays are specified for elevation up to 50° (ADD010, 50 m circle) and 85° with single-site location (ADD011). ADD597 is dual-polarised (V 20 MHz-8.5 GHz, H 20 MHz-7.5 GHz). Interpretation: rings at different heights cover different bands. Elevation comes from a 2-D correlation on a planar ring, and is specified where a planar ring does it well (skywaves at high elevation). Source: R&S Radiomonitoring and Radiolocation Catalog 2007/2008, ch. 3 Direction Finders (rohde-schwarz.com; PDF fetched earlier in this session, text at /private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-ascalva--claude-spares-spare-iFcbz1/8b6bf486-3081-448c-8f46-b00ba3ebceb0/scratchpad/array3d/txt/rs_catalog.txt) ; https://www.rohde-schwarz.com/product/add253.html ; https://www.rohde-schwarz.com/product/add597.html ; https://www.rohde-schwarz.com/product/ddf550.html
- **KrakenSDR guidance** (high): KrakenSDR recommends a uniform circular array with spacing multiplier s between 0.2 and 0.5 (typically 0.33), a good ground plane, and mounting as high as possible away from obstructions. Its wiki discusses bearings only; elevation is not mentioned. When several arrays are used, it suggests spreading them out vertically, for example a smaller array stacked on a larger one. Source: https://github.com/krakenrf/krakensdr_docs/wiki/04.-Antenna-Array-Setup ; https://github.com/krakenrf/krakensdr_docs/wiki/03.-Direction-Finding-Background-Theory
- **Polarisation diversity and vector sensors** (medium): Arrays of diversely polarised elements can find the direction of arbitrarily polarised sources and estimate their polarisation (Ferrara and Parks 1983; performance analysed by Weiss and Friedlander 1991). A single co-located EM vector sensor (3 dipoles + 3 loops) estimates azimuth and elevation with no aperture (Nehorai and Paldi 1994; Wong and Zoltowski 1997, ESPRIT with one vector sensor). Commercial DF antennas offer H-pol as well as V-pol (R&S ADD597, ADD557SR). Application (estimate): array16's whips and panel are V-pol only, and height offsets add no polarisation diversity. Tilting elements would, but makes the manifold depend on polarisation (two more unknowns per source) and doubles the calibration. A 6-component vector sensor needs more coherent channels than array16's four. Source: https://doi.org/10.1109/TAP.1983.1143038 ; https://doi.org/10.1109/78.134397 ; https://doi.org/10.1109/78.275610 ; https://doi.org/10.1109/8.633852 ; https://www.rohde-schwarz.com/product/add557sr.html ; https://www.rohde-schwarz.com/product/add597.html
- **Tetrahedral / multi-face 3-D arrays in practice** (high): Luo, Zhang, Yang and Guan (Sensors 2022) built an all-directional regular tetrahedral array of four synchronised UWB transceivers. They processed it as four planar sub-arrays, each solved as a UCA, and needed a TDoA-based cost function to resolve phase ambiguity. In practice, 3-D arrays for full coverage are processed face by face and need extra information to resolve ambiguity. Source: https://doi.org/10.3390/s22041532
- **Conformal and multi-face arrays: practice and costs** (medium): Fixed-face radars use several faces for wide azimuth coverage. AN/SPY-1 uses four antennas for 360°. PAVE PAWS has two faces, each steering ±60° (120° per face), covering 240°. Josefsson and Persson (Wiley-IEEE Press 2006) is the standard reference on conformal arrays. Its costs (from my knowledge of the book, not re-verified here): only the part of the array facing a direction contributes, embedded element patterns differ across the array, and polarisation varies with element orientation. All of these apply to a multi-tile panel. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/SPY-1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PAVE_PAWS ; https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Conformal+Array+Antenna+Theory+and+Design-p-9780471465843
- **Panel (2.3-6 GHz): multi-face vs one tile (estimate)** (medium): Assuming the cos^1.3 element power pattern used in experiments/array3d_study.py: 3 tiles at ±60° lose 3.9 dB at the face edges, with the beam twice as broad; 4 tiles at ±45° lose 2.0 dB, with the beam 1.4x as broad. At $523 per tile (REDESIGN section 10), the extra tiles add $1,046-1,569. Each direction still sees one 16-element tile, and with four radio channels the faces must be switched. A turntable gives 360° in sequence. The panel's tile ground is vertical (V-pol cards in vertical planes), so its boresight is horizontal and it already measures elevation near the horizon within its ±60° field. At 2.3-6 GHz the panel and the whip plate together form a two-plane 3-D array, since the core can mix tile quadrants and external ports across channels. - **GA-optimised 3-D arrays** (medium): The GA array-geometry work I could verify is planar: Haupt 1994 (thinned arrays) and Haupt 2008 (element spacing of low-sidelobe concentric ring arrays). Gera and Mulgrew (EUSIPCO 2009) relocate elements of a planar array to shape its CRB. I could not verify any GA-optimised 3-D DF array paper because the search budget ran out. In this project, the GA (evolve, climb and random_search with reselect) in a 500 x 500 x 300 mm volume found 3-D layouts with the same azimuth as the GA-planar layouts and about 8x better elevation near the horizon: el p95 0.62 vs 4.93° for 0.4-2.3 GHz, 0-10° up. Simple shapes did as well (stacked rings 0.57°). Source: https://doi.org/10.1109/8.299602 ; https://doi.org/10.1109/TAP.2007.913176 ; /Users/ascalva/Documents/code/sdr_array/array16/experiments/array3d/run.log
- **Model errors and calibration cost of going 3-D** (medium): In practice, model errors and mutual coupling limit DF accuracy more than noise (Swindlehurst and Kailath 1992; Friedlander and Weiss 1991). Estimate: the 3° per-whip residual alone makes azimuth about 6x worse than the noise CRB (0.048 to 0.29° at 1 GHz). A 3-D layout multiplies the calibration work. The manifold must be measured over azimuth and elevation: four elevations means four times the turntable work. Raised whips are no longer monopoles on the plate, so they need sleeve dipoles with chokes or their own ground discs. Their feed lines and supports sit inside the array, and their patterns depend on elevation and on the plate below. Source: https://doi.org/10.1109/78.143447 ; https://doi.org/10.1109/8.76322 ; estimate: engineering judgment
- **Transmit steering and radar (estimate)** (low): A 300 mm vertical aperture is at most 1 wavelength below 1 GHz, so elevation steering of the four transmit whips gains at most about 2-3 dB of vertical directivity and costs azimuth aperture. Active radar uses the panel and a horn, where 3-D only means more faces. Passive-radar targets (aircraft) are usually well above the horizon, where the plate's elevation is adequate: at 30° up the elevation CRB is about 1.7x the azimuth CRB (0.111 vs 0.064° at 1 GHz).
