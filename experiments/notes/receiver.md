# Build our own coherent receiver, or buy radios?: research notes (unreviewed)

## research:discrete

Yes, coherent channels can be built from smaller parts, and the parts are cheap (about $50 per channel against $270 per bladeRF channel). Power, host bandwidth, engineering effort and the loss of transmit decide it, not price.

(a) Shared LO. One synthesizer (LMX2572, 12.5 MHz-6.4 GHz, 0.25 W, $7-16) drives every demodulator through resistive splits and GVA-84+ amplifiers (DC-7 GHz, 0.53 W, $2.2, one per four channels). The LO's random phase after a retune, and its phase noise, are common to all channels and cancel. Channel-to-channel phase then becomes a fixed function of frequency that a stored table covers. Two AD9361s re-randomise on every tune (even with an external LO they land at 0 or 180 deg, set by the LO input divider), and each R820T2 has its own PLL. Use polyphase-quadrature demodulators (ADL5380); divide-by-2 parts (ADL5387) bring the 0/180 ambiguity back. What still drifts:
- LO line length (0.7 deg per 10 C at 6 GHz for 100 mm)
- LNA, filter and amplifier phase with temperature and gain state
- I/Q imbalance and DC offset
- baseband-filter delay mismatch (2.5 ns gives 4.5 deg at 5 MHz)
- ADC skew (the AFE5832LP samples paired inputs 12.5 ns apart)
The rev B injection network stays, but for drift tracking rather than after every retune.

Power is the blocker. Wideband demodulators are base-station parts drawing 1.2-2.4 W each at 5 V (ADL5380 245 mA, TRF371125 360 mA, LTC5586 ~440 mA, LTC5594 470 mA). Add an AFE5832LP digitising 32 inputs (24.4 mW each, ~$214) plus an FPGA and USB 3:
- 8 channels: 13.5 W at the rails, 15.9 W from USB-C, ~$420 in parts
- 16 channels: 25 W at the rails, 29 W from USB-C, ~$770 in parts
Both figures come before the rev B front end (6-7.3 W). Sixteen channels of 10 MSPS 16-bit I/Q is 640 MB/s, more than one USB 3 link carries.

Low IF moves the signal off DC, so DC offset, LO leakage and 1/f noise are removed digitally. I/Q imbalance turns into image leakage instead: 25 dB at 1 dB or 6.5 deg, 40 dB at 0.1 dB and 1 deg, and 60 dB only at 0.01 dB and 0.1 deg. Calibration therefore has to measure and correct it. In an array the image carries the conjugate steering vector, which shows up as a ghost at the mirror bearing.

(b) Tuner bank. KrakenSDR ($749; five R820T2+RTL2832U; 24-1766 MHz; 8-bit; 2.56 MHz; 11 W) gets coherence from three things:
- one 1-ppm clock
- a noise source behind high-isolation switches
- heimdall (GPL-3.0, 64-bit Linux): FFT cross-correlation, per-RTL sample-rate trims, eigenvector phase calibration to 3 deg, recalibration on every retune
Its hardware is unpublished. The R828D is gone (Blog V4 end-of-line); the R860, a renamed R820T2, continues (Blog V3 $34.95). Sixteen tuners need 77-82 MB/s against USB 2.0's practical 40 MB/s. That means three or four separate buses, or tuners feeding a multichannel ADC and FPGA, as the 8-channel CR-8 does (R860s, Microchip 8-input ADC, 12-bit, 8 MHz, USB 3, <1 deg, target $500-600). Sixteen tuners draw ~9.4 W, ~14 W in all. A shared-LO block downconverter in front reaches 6 GHz and keeps the tuners at a fixed frequency, for ~0.3-0.6 W more per channel. Bandwidth stays at 2.5-8 MHz, which hurts radar.

(c) Verified examples: KrakenSDR (5 channels), CR-8 (8), QuadRF (4, 4.9-6 GHz, MAX2850/MAX2851), FMCOMMS5 (2x AD9361). The shared web-search budget ran out before university 8-16-channel builds were found.

### Findings

- **Coherence: AD9361 baseline** (high): The AD9361 has no mechanism to phase-align multiple chips. With internal LOs the phase is random per LO change, sample-rate change, some gain changes and quadrature tracking. With a shared external LO (ADF5355) it is still random but limited to 0 or 180 deg, because the external-LO input divider cannot be bypassed. It re-randomises after power cycles, LO dropouts and large frequency steps. Source: https://analogdevicesinc.github.io/system-level/solutions/reference-designs/fmcomms5/phase-sync/
- **Coherence: demodulator quadrature method** (medium): ADL5380 (400 MHz-6 GHz) generates LO quadrature with a polyphase filter, so it takes LO = RF and needs LO harmonic filtering. ADL5387 uses a divide-by-2 phase splitter fed with 2x LO (100 MHz-4 GHz). By analogy with the AD9361 input divider, a divider's start state can give a 0/180 deg offset between chips; this is an inference, not a datasheet statement. ## research:buy

Short answer for this lens: yes, there are other tools, but nearly every radio that reaches 6 GHz for under about $2,000 uses the same Analog Devices AD936x transceiver as the bladeRF. Switching changes the price, the host link and the timing hooks, but not the core problem. Each chip's LO phase comes back random after a retune (UHD's sync guide says each LO "may have a random phase offset" and must be re-aligned "after each tune"). So rev B's shared 10 MHz plus a PN calibration after every retune is needed with any of them, and that design carries over unchanged.

Four coherent channels at 0.4-6 GHz, November 2026 (two radios unless noted):
- **2x bladeRF 2.0 micro xA4: $1,080.** $540 each, in stock at Nuand (it launched at $480 in 2018; the $670 xA5 is out of stock and Nuand points buyers to the xA4 or xA9). In-spec AD9361, 56 MHz, 61.44 MSPS, USB 3. REFIN locks to nearly any reference up to 300 MHz; it also has CLKOUT to CLKIN and the J51-1 trigger. libbladeRF 2025.10 has Homebrew builds for current Apple-silicon macOS. It is still the cheapest option that is documented, in stock and runs natively on a Mac.
- **2x LibreSDR B220 mini (a Chinese B210 clone): about $620-840.** XC7A200T FPGA plus a claimed AD9361, USB 3, 10 MHz + PPS, runs UHD with a third-party FPGA image. It saves $240-460 and gets UHD's PPS-timed start. But listings disagree on AD9361 versus AD9363, the image is "as-is", and vendors claim only Windows/Linux. Credible only after testing one unit.
- **2x Pluto+ or Zynq LibreSDR (about $360-640), or 2x ADALM-PLUTO Rev D in 2r2t mode (about $450-480): not credible for rev B as specified.** The AD9363 is guaranteed only to 3.8 GHz / 20 MHz; ADI says that beyond that "performance is not guaranteed". The Pluto's second channel is not production-tested, and USB 2 sustains about 4 MSPS. Two channels at rev B's 20 MSPS need 1.28 Gbit/s, which is more than Gigabit Ethernet (reports show 6-20 MSPS total per board). There is no documented trigger or PPS input, so the 1023-chip PN code (+/-256 us) would have to grow (2^15-1 chips gives +/-8 ms).
- **More expensive with no gain for rev B:** ANTSDR E200 AD9361 ($687-727 each), ANTSDR U220 (about EUR 854), LiteX-M2SDR (EUR 699 and sold out; best timing with 10 MHz/PPS/PTP/White Rabbit, but Linux-first), USRP B210 ($2,387 each), B206mini-i ($1,820, 1x1), four HackRF Pros ($1,600, half-duplex, 8-bit).
- **Ruled out by frequency range:** LimeSDR Mini 2.0 (to 3.5 GHz, 1x1), LimeSDR XTRX and LimeSDR Micro (to 3.8 GHz), KrakenSDR (5 RX, 24-1766 MHz, $749, receive-only).
- **HackRF successor:** the HackRF Pro is $400 at SparkFun. It is still one half-duplex channel (20 MSPS, USB 2.0) and now has clock and trigger SMAs. Rev B's "$340 HackRF One" line should read about $400.

8-16 coherent channels under about $1,500:
- **Nothing turnkey reaches 6 GHz.**
- **Below 1.766 GHz, yes:** KrakenSDR (5 channels), or a DIY RTL-SDR array (Aalto ran 21 coherent channels; dongles are $37.95).
- **4.9-6.0 GHz only:** QuadRF tiles, 4 coherent TX/RX per tile on MAX2850/MAX2851 mixers. The $499 kit includes a Pi 5; six bare tiles cost $594 and ship January 2027.
- **To 6 GHz:** 8 RX means four AD936x boards. That is $720-1,280 as Pluto-class clones (with the caveats above), $1,240-1,680 as B210 clones, or $2,160 as bladeRFs. 16 RX means eight boards, at least $1,440 for the radios alone.
- **Cheapest turnkey 8-channel phase-coherent radio to 6 GHz found:** the RFSoC-based PZSDR P047 at $8,749.

Bottom line: keep 2x bladeRF xA4 ($1,080) as the baseline. The only real saving is a pair of B210 clones, and only after one unit has been tested. Don't plan on 8-16 channels at 6 GHz for under $1,500.

## research:effort

The radio can be re-implemented, and people have done it, but at quantity two it is the most expensive, slowest and riskiest part of array16 to rebuild. The software analogy breaks on three numbers: a hardware iteration takes 1.5-3 weeks, costs hundreds of dollars, and a bad one can destroy a $300+ chip.

Histories. Funded professionals needed about two years and several boards: HackRF went from separate prototype boards (June 2012) through the DARPA-funded Jawbreaker beta (slipped to February 2013 over clock-chip sourcing) to HackRF One in 2014, then revisions r6-r10 (2020-2024) mostly for parts availability. LimeSDR slipped two to three months when end-of-life parts forced a redesign; shipped rev 1.4 boards ran hot enough to burn fingers and lost HF to a matching inductor. LimeSDR Mini 2.0 was respun onto another FPGA during the shortage. XTRX and KrakenSDR shipped months late and KerberosSDR missed its date, like about 75% of Kickstarter tech projects. The one well-documented solo AD936x board, FreeSRP, took a teenager two years and three revisions (regulator errors, a wrong feedback resistor that killed the RFIC, a reflow-oven failure costing $400 of parts, clocks on the wrong FPGA pins, weak transmit). Henrik Forsten's 2024 discrete 6 GHz radar (Zynq, DDR3, six layers) cost $330 for two assembled boards plus EUR 225 of parts; one of the two worked, and its design worked around DDR overshoot, FPGA clock jitter, fractional-N spurs and LO leakage (-21 dBc before digital cancellation).

Money. The AD9361 alone lists at $300-415 from authorised distributors, 55-77% of a $540 bladeRF xA4. My estimate for two self-built AD9361+FPGA+FX3 boards is $1,800-2,450 for the first spin (with US import duty) against $1,080 for two bladeRFs, plus $900-1,300 per respin. Chinese clones at $100-280 sell for less than their chips cost at distributors, which points to grey-market silicon (Forsten's $17 Zynq against $173 at Digi-Key; a 2026 LibreSDR owner could not verify the board's AD9361). Fabrication is not the obstacle: JLCPCB builds 6-20 layer boards with free filled via-in-pad, +/-10% TDR-checked impedance and BGA assembly with automatic X-ray ($1.64 a board) in 4+ days. Import duty is the new cost: JLCPCB prepays 35% of order value on US PCB and assembly orders (since March 2026), which could also add up to about $410 to rev B's own core and tile (not in REDESIGN section 10).

Effort (estimate): a bladeRF-class clone is 400-1,300 hours solo, against 520 hours for all of rev B. At 17-35 spins a year at most, "mutate and keep what works" loops belong in simulation and calibration, not PCB spins; budget two or three spins (professionals average 2.9).

Test gear: about $600-1,000 minimum (tinySA Ultra+ to 7.3 GHz, about EUR 159; LiteVNA-64, EUR 145-192, above REDESIGN's $120; scope, current-limited supply, hot air, microscope). A calibrated look at the 11.6 GHz harmonic needs a used 8563E ($1,900-5,000) or a borrowed one. Home BGA works for practised builders but cannot be inspected without X-ray; with a $350 chip, pay for assembly.

Regulation: a home-built device (five or fewer, not marketed, not a kit) needs no FCC authorisation (15.23) but should meet Part 15 "to the greatest extent practicable". Tuning 400-960 MHz puts it in the regulated receiver class (15.101): at most 2 nW (-57 dBm) at the antenna port (15.111), Class B limits of 200/500 uV/m at 3 m (about -49/-41 dBm EIRP, 15.109), and no harmful interference (15.5). Selling kits would end the exemption.

Rebuilding pays where no product exists: front ends, switching, calibration and clock distribution, which rev B already builds. Search budget ran out before PCBWay quotes and door-to-door lead times were checked.

## research:datapath

Short answer for the digital side: yes. Every digital layer of a bladeRF can be rewritten or replaced, and most of it already exists as open code. Doing it yourself does not save money for a single unit, though, and each hardware round takes weeks instead of seconds.

A bladeRF 2.0 micro xA4 ($540, in stock at Nuand) is three chips. An AD9361 handles the RF and the 12-bit converters and has its own decimating filters. A Cyclone V FPGA does the LVDS capture and framing; about 32 of its 49 kLE are free. An FX3 chip bridges to USB3. Its HDL and FX3 firmware are MIT-licensed and libbladeRF is LGPL-2.1. The AD9361 driver inside it is ADI's BSD-3 no-OS code; ad9361.c alone is 7,813 lines. LiteX (BSD-2) works like a package ecosystem for FPGAs. LiteEth generates a UDP streaming core from a YAML file. LiteX-M2SDR is a complete open AD9361 + Artix-7 design: a PRBS-calibrated LVDS interface, 8/12-bit packing, VRT packets, and PCIe, Ethernet and USB3 transports.

**Bandwidth comes first.** I/Q rate = channels x MS/s x 2 x bits/8.
- Rev B's 4 channels x 20 MS/s x 16-bit = 320 MB/s.
- 4 channels at 61.44 MS/s = 492 to 983 MB/s.
- 16 channels x 20 MS/s x 12-bit = 960 MB/s.

What each link carries in practice:
- USB 2.0: about 40 MB/s.
- FX3 USB3: 391 MB/s measured, against a 403 MB/s bus limit. One bladeRF therefore cannot move 2 x 61.44 MS/s at 16-bit (492 MB/s), which is why libbladeRF offers 12-bit packed and 8-bit formats.
- FT601: about 350-360 MB/s.
- USB3 built into the FPGA itself (usb3_pipe, 2026): 446 MB/s on Artix-7, tested on Linux only.
- 1 GbE: 120-124 MB/s. 10 GbE: about 1.2 GB/s. PCIe Gen2 x4: about 1.75 GB/s. Thunderbolt 4: 32 Gb/s of PCIe.

**Mac specifics.** USB (libusb) and Ethernet need no driver. PCIe over Thunderbolt needs a DriverKit extension with entitlements Apple must grant, or System Integrity Protection (SIP) turned off. ThunderScope's permissively licensed LitePCIe DriverKit driver shows it can be done. Vivado (Xilinx's tool) and Quartus (Intel/Altera's tool, needed for the bladeRF HDL) run only on x86 Windows or Linux. Yosys, nextpnr, Trellis and Apicula run natively on Apple silicon, and openXC7 builds on macOS.

**Measured on this M2 Pro.** Python UDP receive tops out at about 100-150k datagrams/s. That is enough for 1 GbE (81k frames/s) but not for 10 GbE. A numpy digital downconverter (NCO + 64-tap FIR, decimate by 8) uses 0.59 of one core for 4 x 20 MS/s, 1.8 cores for 4 x 61.44 and 2.4 cores for 16 x 20. So decimate on the host unless the link is the bottleneck. Doing it in the FPGA for 16 channels needs about 40-100 multipliers; the largest ECP5 has 156.

**FPGA choices.**
- ECP5: fully open toolchain on the Mac; LVDS only on the left and right I/O banks, at 624-800 Mb/s.
- Artix-7: 950-1,250 Mb/s LVDS and the most ready-made IP, but needs Vivado on x86 Linux.
- Zynq-7000: ADI HDL plus Linux IIO; the 7020 chip costs about $105-145.
- Gowin GW5A: 1.6 Gb/s LVDS and a $200 board with 10 GbE SFP+; open-toolchain support is still in progress.

**Effort (estimates).**
- Capture -> packets -> 1 GbE -> Python on a LiteX dev board: 180-470 h.
- The same over FX3 USB3: 250-650 h.
- Over PCIe/Thunderbolt to macOS: 300-860 h.
- Decimation in the FPGA adds 60-150 h, against 10-20 h on the host.
- A custom board with two AD9361s (your own bladeRF pair): about 600-1,400 h and $1,000-1,400. Rev B step 0 needs $1,080 and 40 h, and one AD9361 chip alone costs about $354.

**Hardest parts for a software engineer:**
- Passing data between clock domains.
- LVDS timing: AD9361 pair skew is up to 0.9 ns on a 4.07 ns clock.
- Meeting timing, and debugging without printf.
- BGA and high-speed PCB layout.
- Toolchain and driver friction on the Mac.

**Where re-implementing pays off:**
- Gateware on the bladeRFs you buy (MIT-licensed).
- DSP on the host.
- A custom capture board, but only if you want more simultaneous channels than the bladeRFs give. The digital side of 16 channels is modest; the RF side is the expensive part.

### Findings

- **Data rates** (high): Rev B's own load is 4 ch x 20 MS/s x 16-bit I/Q = 320 MB/s (160 MB/s per bladeRF). At 61.44 MS/s the same 4 channels need 491.5 MB/s (8-bit), 737.3 (12-bit packed) or 983.0 MB/s (16-bit). ### Open questions

- Does usb3_pipe's FPGA-native USB3 device (446 MB/s on Artix-7) work with a macOS xHCI host and libusb? It is documented only against Linux.
- Can two bladeRFs sustain 4 x 20 MS/s SC16 (320 MB/s total, two ports) on this M2 Pro without drops? No macOS benchmark was found; measure it in rev B build step 0, along with whether SC16_Q11_PACKED or SC8 is needed above about 40 MS/s per board.
- Python UDP receive was measured only on loopback. A real Thunderbolt or USB Ethernet adapter on macOS (no recvmmsg or kernel bypass) may be slower; test before relying on 1 GbE in pure Python.
- Would Apple grant the DriverKit PCI entitlement to a hobbyist without a PCI-SIG vendor ID? If not, a PCIe/Thunderbolt capture board means running with SIP disabled.
- FPGA and bridge-chip prices for November 2026 could not be confirmed first-hand: Digi-Key, Mouser and Octopart blocked fetching and the aggregator snapshots are undated. Dev-board prices (ECP5/Artix-7 boards with GbE) were not checked because the shared web-search budget ran out. A follow-up can continue the search.
- Does the owner want more than 4 simultaneous channels? 16 simultaneous channels would remove Hadamard switching, and the digital side handles it (up to about 1 GB/s, or decimate in the FPGA to fit 1-2.5 GbE), but the 16 RF downconverters are outside this lens.
- Is the bladeRF xA4's ~32 kLE of free logic enough for in-FPGA decimation or PN correlation on both channels at 61.44 MS/s? This needs a Quartus fit test on an x86 Linux machine.
- Can an M.2 SDR such as LiteX-M2SDR run on a Mac in a Thunderbolt M.2 enclosure? Many enclosures expect NVMe, and the M2SDR host software is Linux-only today.
- How usable is the GW5A open toolchain (Apicula), and does Gowin's vendor tool run on macOS?
